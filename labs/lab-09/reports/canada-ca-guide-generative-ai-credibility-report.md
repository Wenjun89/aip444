## 2. What the document is

A ~9,000-word operational guidance document addressed to **federal institutions and public servants**, structured into: Overview · What is generative AI? · Challenges and opportunities · Recommended approach · Policy considerations and best practices · Use of this guide · Additional support · Frequently asked questions.

Its core content:
- A definition of generative AI built on the existing **Directive on Automated Decision-Making**;
- A risk inventory (inaccurate output, bias, IP/privacy violations, loss of human oversight, environmental cost, exploitative data-labelling labour);
- The **"FASTER" principles** (Fair, Accountable, Secure, Transparent, Educated, Relevant);
- Concrete best practices (e.g., do not input personal information into publicly available tools; disclose AI use; assess impact of inaccurate outputs);
- A companion one-page summary, [Generative AI in your daily work](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai/generative-ai/generative-ai-your-daily-work.html), plus training via the Canada School of Public Service.

It is **guidance, not law** — it states it "complements and supports compliance with" existing federal laws and policies on privacy, security, IP and human rights.

## 3. Dimension-by-dimension analysis

### 3.1 Authorship — institutional, verifiable, but anonymous at the individual level
- **No individual author is named.** This is standard for Government of Canada policy instruments: the accountable publisher is the institution, not a byline.
- The page does provide an **official contact channel**: TBS's Responsible Data and AI team (`ai-ia@tbs-sct.gc.ca`), plus the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security's `ITSAP.00.041` as supporting guidance.
- Institutional competence is documented on TBS's own [mandate page](https://www.canada.ca/en/treasury-board-secretariat/corporate/mandate.html): TBS "provides guidance to help departments effectively implement government policies and priorities" and "sets the direction for how money and technology are managed by departments."
- **Risk noted:** no named subject-matter experts, so individual expertise and conflicts of interest cannot be assessed. Mitigated, but not eliminated, by the institutional accountability chain.

### 3.2 Publication — official federal outlet; expert/institutional review, not peer review
- Hosted on **canada.ca**, the official Government of Canada website, inside the TBS "Responsible use of artificial intelligence in government" section.
- Third-party validation of authenticity and status:
  - **Osler, Hoskin & Harcourt LLP**, *AI in Canada: A legal guide* (2025), cites the TBS **"FASTER" principles** and the generative AI guide as the framework for federal public sector AI use.
  - **Global Government Forum** reported on the guidelines' release, including stakeholder reaction.
  - **GCwiki** (Government of Canada collaborative wiki), **Digital Government Hub**, and **regulations.ai** all index it as official federal guidance.
  - TBS news releases (e.g., the 2025 AI Strategy launch) direct public servants to the guide.
- **Editorial process:** not peer-reviewed. It is *editor-reviewed* in the governmental sense — the page states version 2 "incorporates feedback from internal stakeholders and external experts," and ISED's [Advisory Council on AI meeting summaries](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/ised/en/advisory-council-artificial-intelligence/meeting-summaries-advisory-council-artificial-intelligence) record TBS presenting drafts of the generative-AI guidance to the Council for feedback.
- **Funding/sponsorship:** none disclosed and none plausibly distorting — a federal central agency publishing its own guidance, with no advertising or sponsor content.

### 3.3 Evidence and citations — above average for policy guidance
- The text carries **dozens of numbered inline footnotes** (fn1 through at least fn25/26 were observed in retrieved snippets) and hyperlinks to primary instruments: the Directive on Automated Decision-Making, privacy/security guidance, and ITSAP.00.041.
- **Two spot-checks of substantive claims passed:**
  1. *Claim:* data-labelling/annotation "is often outsourced to countries where workers are paid very low wages." → **Corroborated** by TIME's investigation (Jan. 2023) into OpenAI's Kenyan data workers via Sama, paid roughly **$1.32–$2.00/hour**.
  2. *Claim:* "ChatGPT-3.5 is trained on data up to early 2022." → **Consistent** with OpenAI's published model documentation for GPT-3.5.
- The document behaves as a **primary source**: it is itself the authoritative statement of the Government of Canada's position, rather than reporting on someone else's position.

### 3.4 Corroboration and contradiction
- **Corroborated:** existence, authorship, FASTER framework, and application to federal institutions are confirmed by an independent law firm (Osler), trade press (Global Government Forum), government-adjacent wikis, and policy trackers.
- **Contradicted:** **no** credible source was found contradicting the guide's factual claims.
- Two legitimate critiques exist, both about **adequacy/enforcement rather than accuracy**:
  - **PIPSC president Jennifer Carr** (Global Government Forum) said her union "had not been properly consulted" and that the guide only proposes the government "be careful," which she called "a very subjective term" insufficient to delineate good and bad practice. She also flagged concern about automated decisions affecting benefits and about upskilling employees.
  - Reporting notes there are **no direct penalties** attached to violating the guidance itself (obligations instead flow from existing statutes such as the Privacy Act).
  - Relatedly, a **compliance-gap signal rather than a factual error**: KPMG Canada survey data reported by the *Financial Post* indicates many public sector workers use public tools like ChatGPT for work despite the caution the guide urges.

### 3.5 Bias and tone
Tone is **neutral, advisory and risk-weighted**. Notable for balance, the document:
- Explicitly inventories harms alongside benefits (hallucination, bias, IP/privacy risk, automation bias, environmental cost, exploitative labour);
- Warns that "AI tools aren't the best choice in every situation" and that institutions "should evaluate the tools for their potential to help employees, not replace them";
- Directs institutions to **engage bargaining agents, legal counsel, privacy/security experts and clients** before high-risk deployments;
- Includes a dedicated FAQ addressing practical objections (e.g., what to disclose when using generative AI).
There is a mild pro-adoption framing ("should explore potential uses"), which aligns with the government's 2025 AI Strategy — a policy position rather than a concealed bias, and stated openly.

### 3.6 Currency
- **Original publication:** fall 2023. The University of Windsor Law Library records "last modified Sept 6, 2023."
- **Current version:** the page self-identifies as the **"second version"**; TESL Ontario and regulations.ai both date version 2 to **12 February 2024**; GCwiki describes it as "originally published in Fall 2023 and updated in Winter 2024."
- The page commits to being "updated regularly to keep pace with regulatory and technological change," and it is **still cited as live guidance in late 2026** (a November 2026 industry report cites it as accessed 9 Nov 2026).
- **Limitation:** the page's own `Date modified` footer **could not be captured** — fetched content was truncated before the footer, and the `web.archive.org` retrieval returned **HTTP 403**. A third-party tracker's claim of a Sept 2026 modification is **unverified** and was not relied upon.
- **Caveat on substance:** some examples are inherently time-bound (ChatGPT-3.5 as the reference LLM). The framework remains current; tool-specific details will lag the state of the art.

## 5. Limitations of this evaluation

1. The guide's **references list (endnotes) and `Date modified` footer were not fully retrievable** due to text truncation in retrieval; endnote numbering was confirmed only through search-result excerpts (fn1–fn26).
2. Archived retrieval failed (403), so version dating relies on **triangulated secondary sources** rather than the page's own metadata.
3. Some third-party trackers encountered (e.g., regulations.ai, lavawall.com) are low-authority AI-governance aggregators; their claims about specific modification dates were **not** treated as verified fact.
4. Named-expert credentials could **not** be assessed because none are credited.

## 6. Final verdict

**HIGH credibility.** This is the authoritative, primary Government of Canada guidance on generative AI use in the federal public service, published by the agency that owns federal technology-management policy. Its claims are footnoted, internally consistent, independently corroborated by a major Canadian law firm and reputable trade press, and — where spot-checked against external reporting — accurate. Its tone is candid about AI's risks, and it does not suppress counterarguments; it actively lists the technology's downsides and warns against overuse.

**Recommended use:** appropriate to cite as authoritative for what Canadian federal policy *is* and *recommends* on generative AI. Because it is non-binding guidance rather than law, pair it with the [Directive on Automated Decision-Making](https://www.tbs-sct.canada.ca/pol/doc-eng.aspx?id=32592) and the Government of Canada AI Register for the operative compliance picture, and check the live page's `Date modified` for the exact version before citing version-specific details.

**Residual risks (do not undermine the core verdict):**
- No named individual authors → individual expertise/COI not assessable.
- Exact modification date unverified in this evaluation.
- Enforceability criticized by the largest federal unions (PIPSC), and reported survey data suggests uneven compliance in practice.
- Tool-specific examples (e.g., ChatGPT-3.5) are dated relative to current model generations.
